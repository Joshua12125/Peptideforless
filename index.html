Documentation you can check for yourself

Peptides For Less supplies materials for laboratory research. This page explains how our batch documentation works, what each part of a certificate of analysis (COA) means, and how to check a report against the vial in your hands.

How a report reaches you

From the laboratory to your bench, in five steps.

  1. Independent analysis. A sample is sent to a third-party laboratory. The lab runs its own tests, separate from the seller.
  2. Report issued. The laboratory issues a report under its own name, with its own report ID and test date.
  3. Published in the library. The COA is added to the COA library on the main site. Results are also listed on the Lab Results page.
  4. You check it. Match the batch reference, compound name and quantity to your label. Confirm the laboratory is named and the report has an ID and a date.
  5. Ask if something is off. If a detail does not match, or no report is listed for your batch, contact the company through the main site.

What each field tells you

The numbers match the illustration above.

1
Laboratory
The independent lab that ran the tests. It should be named on the report and be something you can look up.
2
Report ID
The lab's own reference for this report. It is how you find, or ask the lab for, the original.
3
Batch or sample reference
Ties the report to one batch. If it does not match your label, the report is not for your vial.
4
Test date
When the sample was analysed.
5
Purity, by HPLC
High-performance liquid chromatography separates a sample into its components. The report gives the main compound as a percentage of the sample.
6
Identity, by mass spectrometry
Measures molecular mass. A match with the expected mass for the named compound supports its identity.
7
Quantity
The measured amount per vial, set against the amount printed on the label.

Which tests appear depends on the laboratory and the batch. A COA reports results for one batch only. It is not an approval by any regulator.

Other tests a laboratory may include

Depending on the laboratory and the batch, a report can cover more than purity, identity and quantity. These are the ones you will see most often.

Contaminants

Endotoxin (LAL)

Detects bacterial endotoxin, reported in endotoxin units.

Impurities

Residual solvents

Gas chromatography headspace testing for solvents left over from manufacturing.

Impurities

Heavy metals

ICP-MS screening for trace metals such as lead, mercury, arsenic and cadmium.

Moisture

Water content

Karl Fischer titration measures moisture in lyophilised powder.

A report only covers what was tested. If a test is not listed, it was not run.

What a complete COA should include

Use this as a checklist for any report, ours or anyone else's.

Batch identification

A reference that matches the label on your vial.

Laboratory identification

The testing laboratory named on the report, not just a logo.

Report ID and date

Unique to the report, and something the laboratory can confirm.

Methods named

The analytical method behind each result is stated.

Measured values

Actual numbers for purity, identity and quantity, not just a pass or fail mark.

Public access

The report is published where anyone can read it.

Handling, records and naming

Storage
Research materials are usually supplied as lyophilised powder. Follow the storage guidance on the product page and label, often refrigerated, and keep vials sealed and away from light and heat.
Records
Write down the batch number and report ID for every experiment, and keep a copy of the COA with your lab records.
Naming
Confirm the compound name on the report matches your label. Names and abbreviations vary, so where a CAS number is given, use it to check.

Questions

What does "independently tested" mean?
The testing laboratory is a separate company from the seller. It runs its own analysis and issues the report under its own name.
Where do I find the COA for my batch?
Published reports are in the COA library on the main site, and results are also listed on the Lab Results page.
What is in a COA?
The laboratory, report ID, batch reference, test date, the methods used, and results for purity, identity and quantity. Some reports include other tests as well.
How do I know a report is genuine?
Check that the laboratory is named, that the report has an ID and a date, and that the details match your batch. You can also ask the laboratory to confirm a report ID.
What if my batch has no report yet?
Reports are added as they become available. Contact the company through the main site with your batch reference.
Can results differ between batches?
Yes. Each batch is tested on its own, so read the report for your batch and not one for a different batch.
What does "research use only" mean?
The material is supplied for use in a laboratory. It is not intended for human or veterinary use, and customers must be 21 or older.
Is a COA an approval for any use?
No. It records analytical results for one batch. It is not an approval by any regulator.

Questions about a report?

Use the contact page on the main site and include your batch reference, so the right report can be found quickly.
